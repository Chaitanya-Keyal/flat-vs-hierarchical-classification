\section*{What the numbers show}
\small

Pairs of numbers \textit{a}\,/\,\textit{b} are Gini\,/\,entropy; accuracies are on the test set
unless stated otherwise. \res{rec-both} of the \res{rec-total} recordings have syllables on both
sides of the split, so every accuracy here is optimistic for new recordings (the handout's caveat).

\paragraph{Single tree (Part 1).}
Grown without limits, both trees fit the training set exactly (100\%; depth
\both{dt}{depth}, with \both{dt}{leaves} leaves) but reach only \both{dt}{test}\% on the test
set: a full tree has low bias and high variance. Table~4 shows the trade-off. Shallow trees
underfit both sets; test accuracy levels off by depth~12 while training accuracy goes on to
100\%, so the deepest splits fit noise in the training rows, not structure.
Entropy is ahead of Gini at every depth on this data. The derived family accuracy
(\both{dt}{family}\%) is higher than the species accuracy because a species error inside the
right family still counts as a family hit.

\paragraph{Bagging and AdaBoost (Part 2).}
Bagging 25 trees gains \both{bag}{gain} points over the single tree. Each tree overfits its own
bootstrap sample differently, and the vote averages those errors away (variance reduction);
training accuracy stays at \both{bag}{train}\%, since a row that only a minority of the trees saw
can still be outvoted. AdaBoost attacks bias instead: one depth-3 tree reaches
\both{sweep}{3-test}\% (Table~4), and 50 reweighted rounds lift that to \both{ada}{test}\%, about
the single unconstrained tree (\both{ada}{gain} points). All 50 rounds were kept: the weighted
error grows from \both{ada}{eps-first} to \both{ada}{eps-last} as the weights move onto the hard
rows, but stays below the limit $1 - 1/K = 0.9$. Decision stumps reach only
\both{stumps}{test}\%. A stump has two leaves, so it names at most two of the ten species per
round; its weighted error climbs to \both{stumps}{eps-last}, close to the limit, which leaves each
late stump a small say ($\alpha = \both{stumps}{alpha-last}$, against \both{ada}{alpha-last} for
the last depth-3 trees), and the 50-stump vote predicts only \both{stumps}{species} of the ten
species on the test set.

\paragraph{Random forest (Part 3).}
The forest is the best flat model: \both{rf}{test}\%, \both{rf}{gain-bag} points above bagging and
\both{rf}{gain-dt} above the single tree. Its trees are individually weaker than bagged trees
(mean single-tree accuracy \both{treeacc}{rf}\% against \both{treeacc}{bag}\%), because each split
sees only \res{rf-m} of the 22 MFCCs, but they agree less often: the average pairwise agreement of
their test predictions is \both{agree}{rf}, against \both{agree}{bag} for bagging. Less correlated
errors cancel in the vote, so the ensemble gains more than its members lose, as the handout
predicts.

\paragraph{Hierarchy (Part 4).}
The hierarchy is \both{hier}{loss} points below the flat tree (\both{hier}{test}\% against
\both{dt}{test}\%). The experts are not the problem: given the true family they reach
\both{t3}{oracle}\% (the oracle), and only \both{t3}{pa}\% of the rows are lost by an expert after
correct routing. The router is the bottleneck. It sends \both{t3}{pb}\% of the rows to the wrong
family, each then a certain species error, and its accuracy (\both{t3}{router}\%) is
\both{hier}{router-loss} points below the family accuracy that the flat tree gets for free by
mapping its predicted species: \both{hier}{deficit} test rows overall, and
\both{hier}{large-deficit} in the two large families alone. A likely reason: each family is a
union of species clusters (five in Hylidae, three in Leptodactylidae), which one tree must carve
out from the coarse labels alone. Router recall is lowest on the small families (Bufonidae,
\res{bufo-rows} test rows: \both{hier}{bufo-recall}\%), where the experts are constants and
routing is everything, but the flat tree is weak there too (\both{flat}{bufo-recall}\%). A
perfect router would add \both{hier}{gap} points. On this dataset the easier sub-problems do not make up for the router's
mistakes, so the hierarchy does not help.

\paragraph{Naive Bayes (Part 5).}
Naive Bayes is the weakest model (\res{nb-test}\%). It treats the 22 MFCCs as independent within
each species, but they are not: within a species the mean $|r|$ between two features is
\res{within-min} to \res{within-max}, and over the training set \res{corr-above} of the 231 pairs
have $|r| > 0.5$, up to \res{corr-max}. Correlated features count the same evidence several
times, so a block of related MFCCs can outvote the rest; a tree uses one feature per split and is
not misled by redundancy. A second effect is
specific to this data: MFCC~\res{nb-constant-feature} is constant in every training row of
\res{nb-constant-species}, so its variance there is only $\varepsilon = \res{nb-eps}$. The
\res{nb-constant-shared} test rows that share that value gain a large likelihood bonus for the
species: naive Bayes predicts it for \res{nb-constant-predicted} test rows,
\res{nb-constant-wrong} of them wrongly.
